CLASS 01 · Domain Spine: Dependability

Topics: 5 Attributes (Availability, Reliability, Safety, Security, Resilience), Sources of Failure

My Evidence (V1):

Intelligent Teaching Assistant – Dependability Mapping

I mapped the five dependability attributes to Intelligent Teaching Assistant, a platform that gives students automated feedback on their assignments, labs, and lab exams.

  • Availability: Reachable during peak assignment-deadline and lab-exam windows when many students use the system simultaneously.
  • Reliability: Accurate grading and feedback a wrong answer marked correct is a real failure.
  • Safety: Isolate student submissions so a malicious one cannot harm the system or other users.
  • Security: Protect student submissions, grades, and credentials with proper authentication and encryption.
  • Resilience: Survive outages and traffic spikes; degrade gracefully instead of collapsing.

Risks include service downtime, isolation failure, data corruption, and malicious-input attacks. Strategy: separation of concerns into independent components, isolated execution of untrusted submissions, retry and fallback logic around the feedback engine, and database backups with replication.

CLASS 02 · Reliability Engineering

Topics: Fault → Error → Failure, POFOD, ROCOF, AVAIL Metrics, 8 Commandments

My Evidence (V1):

Intelligent Teaching Assistant — Reliability Assessment

The system I am assessing is Intelligent Teaching Assistant, a platform that gives students automated feedback on their assignments, labs, and lab exams. It is used by students to submit work, by instructors to monitor progress, and by the platform itself to orchestrate feedback. The system follows an uptime-driven design: it must be reachable whenever an assignment deadline or a lab session is in progress, and a failed feedback request must never silently corrupt a student's record.

Intelligent Teaching Assistant has a dynamic usage pattern. Traffic spikes the night before assignment deadlines and during scheduled lab sessions and lab exams. During those windows the system must handle many concurrent submissions and feedback requests without losing or duplicating any of them. Therefore scalability under load and graceful degradation are critical to maintain continuous service.

The most critical dependability attribute for this system is availability. If the platform is down during a lab exam or at an assignment deadline, students cannot submit and the educational mission fails. Reliability is equally important; a duplicated grade or a lost submission directly damages a student's record. Security protects student data and grades, while maintainability lets us push fixes without taking the platform down mid-session.

Reliability Metric Choice — AVAIL

Following the slide's guidance ("System Type & Risk Profile"), Intelligent Teaching Assistant is uptime-critical and repairable, not safety-critical. Therefore I chose AVAIL (Availability %) over POFOD or ROCOF: what matters is the proportion of time the platform is operational during high-demand windows, and the goal is to minimize downtime duration. Target: ≥ 99.5% availability during assignment-deadline and lab-exam windows, with no single failure exceeding 5 minutes of downtime.

Proposed Strategy — Redundancy + Three Lines of Defense

I propose a single architectural decision: run the orchestration layer as two redundant instances behind a load balancer, with the persistent store replicated to a hot standby. This is Redundancy (multiple identical components in parallel), not Diversity, appropriate here because the dominant risk is a single host or process failure, not a shared design fault. The strategy is reinforced by the 3 Lines of Defense from the lecture: Fault Avoidance via code reviews and the 8 Commandments at the orchestration layer; Fault Detection via health-checks and contract tests at every external boundary; Fault Tolerance via the redundant instances + replicated store + retry-and-fallback around the feedback engine. Together these defenses keep AVAIL above target even when one component or upstream provider fails.

WEEK 07–08

CLASS 03 · Safety Engineering

Topics: Hazard → Accident → Damage, ALARP Triangle, Fault Tree Analysis, Safety Case

My Evidence (V1) — Portfolio Task: The Safety Argument

Safety-Critical Component: Intelligent Teaching Assistant Automated Grading on a Lab Exam

For this safety argument I selected the automated grading subsystem of Intelligent Teaching Assistant on a lab-exam submission. This is the most safety-critical component of the platform: a wrong grade here directly damages a student's academic record, which the student cannot easily recover from.

1. Identify the Hazard

Hazard: "Wrong grade issued for a correct submission on a lab exam." The harm is academic: a student who answered correctly is recorded as having failed, with consequences for their GPA and standing.

2. Risk Assessment

A simple matrix defining the Probability and Severity of this hazard:

Probability ↓ / Severity → Low (single question wrong) Medium (whole lab exam wrong) High (course-failing grade)
Low (rare automated edge-case) Acceptable ALARP Intolerable
Medium (ambiguous rubric) ALARP Intolerable Intolerable
High (systematic grader bug) Intolerable Intolerable Intolerable

The realistic operating point is Low Probability × High Severity: rare, but when it happens the harm is course-failing. That cell is Intolerable, so the hazard must be designed out, not merely tested for.

3. Safety Requirement (Shall-Not)

"The system shall not display a final lab-exam grade to the student unless the automated grade and an independent rubric-based deterministic check agree on the same outcome."

Disagreement forces the submission into a manual review queue. This converts a single point of failure into a two-channel check.

4. Visual Proof — Fault Tree

Top Event: "Wrong grade issued on a lab exam." The branches below are the causes; the OR gate makes any one branch sufficient to trigger the hazard, which is why the safety requirement is needed.

Design-In Decision: the Shall-Not requirement above is the protection system that breaks every branch of this tree before the wrong grade is ever shown. Safety is designed in, not tested in.

WEEK 09–10

GROUP PHASE · Security & Resilience + Software Reuse

Topics: Security Assurance Case, Resilience Patterns, Reuse Strategy

My Evidence (V1) — Security, Resilience & Reuse for Intelligent Teaching Assistant

Security Assurance Case. Four components as taught. System Description: gateway-fronted platform, six independent services, only the orchestration layer is externally reachable. Threat Analysis: cheating student, malicious submission, external attacker, insider with stolen account. Design Rationale: defense-in-depth across five layers (Identity, Input, Isolation, Rate-limit, Audit), so each adversary must defeat multiple independent controls. V&V Results: penetration tests, static analysis, dependency scans, and contract tests at every boundary.

Resilience Case. Failure chain: a missing timeout on the feedback call (fault) saturates the request pool (error) and produces indefinite spinners at peak load (failure). A human expert intervenes only when automated defenses are exhausted: when the circuit breaker exceeds its self-recovery budget, the on-call engineer is paged, decides between extending the degraded mode or forcing a manual fail-over, and the decision is logged immutably for post-incident review.

Swiss Cheese Model — Defensive Layers

Three principles from the slide: add more layers of defence, make the defences different so the holes do not line up, and reduce the number of holes.

Front slices are Technical defenses (authentication, input validation, circuit breakers/health probes); the back slice is the Sociotechnical defense (on-call engineer + mandatory post-incident review). Each slice has different holes, so an Active failure or attack arrow cannot pass through all of them on the same axis. When the arrow does get through the technical slices, for example when the breaker exhausts its self-recovery budget, the sociotechnical slice catches it: the on-call engineer takes control (human-in-the-loop), decides between extending degraded mode or forcing a manual fail-over, and the decision is logged immutably for post-incident review.

Reuse Strategy — Position in the Reuse Landscape

Chosen approach: a hybrid of Application Frameworks + Application System Integration, assembled as a Software Product Line (SPL). Justified against the slide's Reuse Planning Factors: development schedule is short (one semester), team skills are limited to a few members so reused components beat hand-rolled ones, software criticality is high (graded academic work), and the application domain (automated student feedback) has mature off-the-shelf options for transcription, sandboxed execution, and feedback engines.

Product Line Architecture (Core vs Specialized)

Layer Components Modification (per slide)
Core Orchestration, identity, store schema, audit log, isolated execution, transcription NOT modified
Configurable Feedback engine wrapper, rate limits, retry policy, model selection Modified & configured
Specialized Rubrics, prompt templates, dashboards, lab timing, course-specific UI Replaced some or ALL

Validation & The Ariane 5 Lesson

The Ariane 5 failure happened because a module reused from the slower Ariane 4 overflowed under higher speeds and the value was never validated. To prevent that here: every reused component is wrapped behind a contract test that runs before every integration build, with range/type checks on every value crossing into the orchestration layer. The Reliable Programming Commandment enforced is "Validate All Inputs": out-of-contract values are rejected at the boundary and the Resilience-Case degraded path takes over instead of producing a silent failure.

Industry Alignment & Bridge to Assignment 6 (Resilience)

Cloud-based platforms (slide 22): feedback, transcription, and sandboxed execution are consumed as cloud services, isolating each reused component so a failure in one cannot cascade into the others. This is what makes the Reuse Strategy a direct enabler of the Resilience Case: the same circuit-breaker and queued degraded path from Assignment 6 only works because each reused service sits behind its own isolation boundary. Reuse here is not a cost-saving shortcut; it is the mechanism that lets Recognition & Resistance act at a single service boundary instead of the whole monolith.

WEEK 11–12

GROUP PHASE · Distributed & Component-Based Engineering

Topics: Distributed Architecture, CBSE, Requires/Provides Interfaces, Failover

CBSE Strategy

1) Position in CBSE — Development with Reuse

Intelligent Teaching Assistant is built through Development with Reuse, not Development for Reuse. The team is not shipping reusable feedback engines or transcription libraries to other projects; it is composing them into a working teaching assistant. The planning factors push the same way: the schedule is fixed to the academic term, the budget cannot absorb building a feedback model from scratch, and market availability of mature feedback, transcription, and sandboxed-execution components is high. Building these in-house would duplicate solved problems and burn the schedule on capabilities that are not the product's differentiator.

2) Component Composition — Requires / Provides Interfaces

The team builds only the orchestration layer end-to-end; every other capability is reached through an explicit Requires / Provides contract so any component can be replaced without rewriting its neighbours.

Component Requires Provides
UI (Edge) Submission API, Identity API Entry & rendering surface to the student
Orchestration Feedback API, Transcription API, Sandbox API, Store API Submission API, Result API
Feedback (reused) Submission payload (validated) Feedback API
Transcription (reused) Audio reference Transcription API
Sandbox (reused) Code artefact, resource caps Sandbox API (bounded execution result)
Store Identity assertion Store API (durable read/write)

3) Validation & Risk Measurement

Integration testing plan. Each Provides interface ships with a contract test that runs before every integration build; the orchestration layer is exercised end-to-end against contract doubles in CI and against the real reused components in a staging pipeline before release. Named risk: a reused component returns an out-of-range value with a success status, the same Ariane-5-style failure carried forward from the Reuse Strategy. Mitigation: Validate All Inputs is enforced at every Provides boundary; out-of-contract values are rejected at the boundary and the Resilience-Case degraded path (Assignment 6) takes over instead of propagating a corrupt result into the gradebook.

4) Industry Alignment & Bridge to the Reuse Strategy

Composable AI is the alignment: feedback and transcription are reached as composable model services behind stable Provides interfaces, not embedded inside the orchestrator. This continues the efficiency goals set in Assignment 7: the Reuse Strategy chose Application System Integration as the landscape position, and CBSE is what makes that integration safe in practice by turning every reused capability into a replaceable, contract-tested component. The schedule and budget savings claimed in Reuse only materialise if integration risk is contained at the boundary, which is exactly what the Requires / Provides discipline above guarantees.

Distributed Architecture Plan (Assignment 9)

1) Position in the Distributed Landscape

Distributed Component Architecture is the chosen model, not flat Client–Server (a single application tier cannot absorb deadline-week submission spikes without over-provisioning every other capability) and not Peer-to-Peer (graded work requires a trusted authority for identity, ordering, and the official result). Distributed Component Architecture lets each capability scale on its own load profile, lets reused services (from the CBSE plan above) sit behind independent boundaries, and keeps a single authoritative gradebook, matching the resource-sharing and peak-load requirements directly.

2) Nodes, Component Distribution & Low-Latency Data Transfer

Node class Components hosted Scales on
Edge UI, static assets, TLS termination Geographic volume
Orchestration Submission API, Result API, session state Active sessions / min
Compute Feedback workers, Transcription workers Queue depth
Sandbox Isolated execution slots (bounded CPU and memory) Concurrent slots
Data Primary and replica store, artefact blob store Durability and read load

Low-latency data transfer. Heavy artefacts (audio, code, large submissions) are written once to the Data tier; Orchestration, Compute, and Sandbox then exchange references, not payloads. Edge terminates close to the student so request round-trips stay short, and Compute and Sandbox are co-located with Data in the same availability zone so worker pulls are local-network reads, not cross-region traffic.

3) Reliability & Security — Failover Plan + Protocol

Failover (network outage / node failure). Stateless tiers (Edge, Orchestration, Compute, Sandbox) sit behind a health-checked load balancer; a failed node drops out within one probe interval and traffic is shifted to healthy peers without restarting the platform. The Data tier runs primary plus replica with synchronous replication for graded results, so no committed grade is lost on failover. A partition between Orchestration and Compute trips the Assignment-6 circuit breaker into the queued degraded path: the UI stays alive, students keep submitting, and feedback is delivered when the link returns. Security protocol in transit: every inter-node link uses TLS 1.3 with mutual authentication, so a compromised network segment cannot impersonate a node or read graded traffic.

4) Industry Alignment & Bridge to Continuity & Resilience

The plan leverages Cloud Computing and SaaS: Compute and Sandbox capacity is added at deadline spikes and released afterwards (on-demand resources, no permanent over-provisioning), and the reused feedback and transcription capabilities from the CBSE and Reuse plans are consumed as SaaS behind their Provides interfaces, with no in-house GPUs to operate. This is what turns the distributed choice into a Continuity & Resilience guarantee: a single node failure is absorbed by a peer, a single service outage is absorbed by the degraded path, and a deadline-week load spike is absorbed by elastic cloud capacity, so the platform stays available end-to-end instead of crashing the moment one component or one network link fails.

WEEK 13–14

Multi-tier & Middleware Strategy

Topics: Multi-Tier Architecture, Middleware, Master–Slave, Microservices

My Evidence (V1):

Tier Choice. Intelligent Teaching Assistant uses a Multi-Tier architecture, not Thin-client (would push all load onto one server and collapse at deadline spikes) and not Fat-client (cannot trust grading logic on a student device). Browsers do only rendering and input validation; servers carry orchestration, grading, and persistence, the same shape e-commerce platforms use to survive bursty traffic.

The Tiers. Presentation Tier: student and instructor web UI for submission forms, dashboards, and feedback views. Application Tier: orchestration service that routes work to the feedback engine, transcription service, and isolated execution environment, and enforces auth and grading rules. Data Tier: primary plus replica store for submissions, grades, and audit logs. Middleware sits between the Application Tier and these independent services as a message broker and API gateway; it queues feedback jobs, retries on transient failure, and translates each service's contract so the Application Tier sees one uniform interface.

Localized Control and Master/Slave. A Data Tier outage must not silently corrupt the UI: the Application Tier returns an explicit "submission queued, grading delayed" state, the UI shows a banner, and the submission is buffered in the middleware queue until the Data Tier recovers, so there is no silent failure and no total collapse. Inside the grading pipeline, the orchestrator is the Master: it owns the job table, assigns work, and tracks deadlines. Sandbox and transcription workers are Slaves: they pull a task, execute, and report back. If a Slave dies mid-task, the Master reassigns, and the student still gets real-time feedback.

Industry Alignment. The Application Tier is decomposed as Microservices (auth, submission, grading, feedback, transcription) so each can scale and deploy independently; grading capacity grows at deadline weeks without redeploying auth, and a new feedback model ships without touching the UI. Isolating tiers is what makes the system maintainable today and adaptable tomorrow: the Presentation Tier can be re-skinned for a mobile client, the Data Tier can migrate stores, and the Application Tier can swap a service, each without rewriting the others.

🏗 Architecture & Engineering

🔄 Software Reuse

Mature open-source components were reused for execution isolation, transcription, persistence, and the user interface; every choice is justified against the risk it reduces.

🌐 Distributed Architecture

Gateway + Independent Services: an orchestration layer routes requests to the feedback engine, transcription service, isolated execution environment, and persistent store.

🧩 Component-Based

Each service is an independently deployable component with a documented interface. Replacing the feedback engine does not touch the user interface.

🔗 Systems of Systems

Composes an isolated execution environment, a transcription service, and an external feedback engine: three independent systems integrated under one product surface.

Unit tests on authentication and grading logic, contract tests at every external boundary, and end-to-end tests for the submission path.

🛡 Hazard Analysis

Top hazard: escape from the isolated execution environment by a malicious submission. Mitigated via stripped, read-only environment, no network egress, resource caps, and an ephemeral workspace.

  • Architecture Pattern: Gateway + Independent Services

❓ What changed in how I think?

"I used to start from the feature and work outward. Now I start from the failure I refuse to allow and work backward. The redundancy plan, the Shall-Not requirement, the Validate-All-Inputs rule, the Swiss Cheese layers, every choice on Intelligent Teaching Assistant is a decision about which risk I am buying down, not which tool I am using."

⚖️ Safety vs Reliability — My understanding

"Reliability asks did the system do what the spec said? Safety asks did the spec ask for the right thing? An automated grader on Intelligent Teaching Assistant can hit 100% reliability by always returning an answer, and still be unsafe if that answer is confidently wrong on a lab exam. Reliability is conformance; safety is freedom from harm, and the spec itself can be the bug."

💼 What I will carry into my career

"The burden of proof sits with the engineer, not the user. I will not ship a system that touches student records, payments, or health without a written Safety Case, a named Reliability metric, and a degraded path I have actually tested. After this course I know how to build each of those, and that is the standard I will hold my own work to from now on."
